## Acetaminophen (Tylenol): dose limits and liver safety
Source: FDA acetaminophen labeling and safety communications; McNeil Tylenol Extra Strength label; Poison Control guidance
Keywords: acetaminophen, tylenol, paracetamol, dose, maximum, liver, overdose, pain, fever, hepatotoxicity, alcohol

For adults, the usual dose is 500 to 1,000 mg every 4 to 6 hours. The over-the-counter Extra Strength label caps use at 3,000 mg in 24 hours, while prescribers sometimes allow up to 4,000 mg; people who drink alcohol regularly or have liver disease are usually told to stay at or below 2,000 mg a day. The most common cause of accidental overdose is taking several products at once, because acetaminophen hides in many cold, flu, sleep, and prescription pain combinations (often written APAP). An overdose can feel fine for the first day while the liver is being damaged, and the antidote, acetylcysteine, works best within about eight hours, so any possible overdose means calling Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or 911 immediately rather than waiting for symptoms. Children are dosed by weight, about 10 to 15 mg per kilogram every 4 to 6 hours, no more than 5 doses in 24 hours.

## Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin): dose limits and who should avoid it
Source: FDA ibuprofen labeling and 2015 NSAID cardiovascular and GI warnings; American College of Gastroenterology guidance
Keywords: ibuprofen, advil, motrin, nsaid, dose, maximum, stomach, ulcer, kidney, pain, fever, inflammation, heart

Over the counter, adults take 200 to 400 mg every 4 to 6 hours with food or milk, with a label maximum of 1,200 mg a day; higher prescription doses should only be used under a clinician's care. NSAIDs can cause stomach irritation and bleeding, raise blood pressure, strain the kidneys, and slightly raise heart attack and stroke risk, especially at high doses and with long use. They are generally best avoided with kidney disease, a history of stomach ulcers or bleeding, heart failure, uncontrolled high blood pressure, or when taking blood thinners such as warfarin, apixaban, or rivaroxaban. They should also be avoided from about 20 weeks of pregnancy onward unless a doctor says otherwise. Ibuprofen can also blunt the heart-protective effect of low-dose aspirin if taken at the same time. For children over 6 months, the dose is about 5 to 10 mg per kilogram every 6 to 8 hours.

## Naproxen (Aleve) and aspirin: dosing and special cautions
Source: FDA naproxen and aspirin labeling; USPSTF 2022 aspirin recommendation; CDC Reye syndrome information
Keywords: naproxen, aleve, aspirin, low-dose aspirin, nsaid, reye syndrome, primary prevention, heart attack, bleeding, children

Over-the-counter naproxen is 220 mg every 8 to 12 hours, with a limit of 660 mg in 24 hours, and it shares the same stomach, kidney, and heart cautions as ibuprofen. Aspirin should never be given to children or teenagers with a viral illness because of the risk of Reye syndrome, a rare but serious condition affecting the brain and liver. For adults who have already had a heart attack or stroke, a daily low-dose aspirin (81 mg) is often recommended, but for people who have never had one, the 2022 USPSTF advises against starting it at 60 or older and says to individualize the decision from 40 to 59 because bleeding risk offsets much of the benefit. No one should start or stop daily aspirin on their own without discussing it with their clinician.

## Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) and other sedating antihistamines
Source: FDA labeling; American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria 2023
Keywords: diphenhydramine, benadryl, antihistamine, sleep aid, allergy, drowsy, older adults, dementia, urinary retention, doxylamine

Adults typically take 25 to 50 mg every 4 to 6 hours for allergy symptoms, with a limit of about 300 mg a day, and it causes marked drowsiness, dry mouth, and slowed thinking. The Beers Criteria advise older adults to avoid it, because it can cause confusion, falls, urinary retention, and constipation, and long-term use is linked to higher dementia risk. It should not be combined with alcohol, sleeping pills, or other sedating drugs. It is not a safe substitute for epinephrine in a severe allergic reaction. Non-sedating options such as cetirizine, loratadine, or fexofenadine are usually better for routine allergies.

## Dangerous drug interactions to know about
Source: FDA drug safety communications; Lexicomp and Micromedex standard interaction references; American Heart Association
Keywords: interaction, drug interaction, warfarin, coumadin, nitrate, sildenafil, viagra, tadalafil, ssri, tramadol, serotonin syndrome, maoi, st john's wort, grapefruit, potassium, ace inhibitor, statin

Some combinations are well known to be dangerous. Warfarin with NSAIDs, aspirin, or certain antibiotics (such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, metronidazole, or fluconazole) markedly raises bleeding risk. Sildenafil, tadalafil, or vardenafil with any nitrate (nitroglycerin, isosorbide) can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure and must not be combined. Two or more drugs that raise serotonin, such as an SSRI or SNRI with tramadol, triptans, linezolid, MAOIs, dextromethorphan, or St. John's wort, can cause serotonin syndrome (agitation, fever, sweating, tremor, fast heartbeat). ACE inhibitors or ARBs with potassium supplements or spironolactone can push potassium dangerously high. Simvastatin and lovastatin interact with grapefruit juice and clarithromycin, raising the risk of muscle damage. Opioids, benzodiazepines, and alcohol together can stop breathing. Because there are thousands of possible interactions, anyone on several medicines should have a pharmacist run their full list.

## Metformin: kidney function, contrast dye, and side effects
Source: FDA 2016 metformin drug safety communication; ADA Standards of Care
Keywords: metformin, glucophage, diabetes, kidney, egfr, contrast, dye, lactic acidosis, b12, nausea

Metformin is first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes and rarely causes low blood sugar by itself. Since 2016, the FDA has allowed its use down to an eGFR of 30, and it should not be started when eGFR is 30 to 45 and should be stopped below 30. It is usually held around the time of iodinated contrast for CT scans in people with reduced kidney function. Common side effects are stomach upset and diarrhea, which often ease with the extended-release form and with taking it with meals, and long-term use can lower vitamin B12, so levels are checked periodically. Rare lactic acidosis, signs of which include unusual muscle pain, trouble breathing, and severe weakness, needs emergency care.

## Fever: when to worry in adults and children
Source: American Academy of Pediatrics; CDC; Infectious Diseases Society of America
Keywords: fever, temperature, infant, baby, child, newborn, thermometer, high fever, fever in adults, febrile, rectal

A fever is a temperature of 100.4 F (38 C) or higher. A baby under 3 months with any fever, measured rectally, needs to be seen in emergency care right away, even if they otherwise look well. For children 3 months to 3 years, call the doctor for a fever of 102.2 F (39 C) or higher or one lasting more than a day or two, and go in urgently for stiff neck, trouble breathing, a rash that doesn't fade when pressed, unusual drowsiness, seizure, or refusal to drink. In adults, fever above 103 F (39.4 C), lasting more than 3 days, or accompanied by severe headache with stiff neck, confusion, trouble breathing, chest pain, or a persistent rash needs prompt medical attention. Fever itself is the body fighting infection and does not need treating for its own sake, but acetaminophen or ibuprofen can help comfort.

## Blood pressure categories and hypertensive crisis
Source: 2017 ACC/AHA Hypertension Guideline
Keywords: blood pressure, hypertension, high blood pressure, systolic, diastolic, bp, hypertensive crisis, readings, 120/80, stage 1, stage 2

Normal blood pressure is below 120 over 80. Elevated is 120 to 129 over less than 80, stage 1 hypertension is 130 to 139 or 80 to 89, and stage 2 is 140 or higher or 90 or higher. A reading above 180 over 120 should be rechecked after five minutes of rest; if it stays high and comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, vision changes, or trouble speaking, that is an emergency and needs 911. If it stays high without symptoms, the person should contact their doctor the same day. Diagnosis rests on repeated readings, ideally including home readings taken seated after resting, not one measurement. Lifestyle changes include lowering sodium, regular exercise, weight loss, the DASH diet, and limiting alcohol, and many people also need medication.

## Blood sugar targets, diabetes tests, and prediabetes
Source: American Diabetes Association Standards of Care in Diabetes
Keywords: blood sugar, glucose, a1c, diabetes, prediabetes, fasting glucose, hemoglobin a1c, type 2, normal range, mg/dl

Fasting blood sugar under 100 mg/dL is normal, 100 to 125 is prediabetes, and 126 or higher on two separate tests is diabetes. An A1c under 5.7% is normal, 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes, and 6.5% or more is diabetes, and a two-hour glucose of 200 or more during a tolerance test also diagnoses it. A typical A1c goal for many adults with diabetes is under 7%, though a clinician may individualize this. Classic symptoms of high blood sugar are increased thirst, frequent urination, blurred vision, and unexplained weight loss. Prediabetes can often be reversed with a roughly 5 to 7% weight loss and regular activity.

## Low blood sugar: the rule of 15
Source: American Diabetes Association; Endocrine Society
Keywords: hypoglycemia, low blood sugar, shaky, sweating, insulin, glucagon, rule of 15, diabetes emergency, glucose tablets

Low blood sugar is below 70 mg/dL, with symptoms such as shakiness, sweating, hunger, fast heartbeat, irritability, and confusion. If the person is awake and able to swallow, give 15 grams of fast carbohydrate, such as 3 to 4 glucose tablets, half a cup of juice or regular soda, or a tablespoon of sugar or honey, then recheck in 15 minutes and repeat if still below 70, followed by a snack once it is up. If they are confused, can't swallow, or are unconscious, don't put food in their mouth. Use glucagon if available and call 911.

## Cholesterol and lipid numbers
Source: 2018 AHA/ACC Cholesterol Guideline
Keywords: cholesterol, ldl, hdl, triglycerides, lipid panel, statin, total cholesterol, heart disease, lipids

A desirable total cholesterol is under 200 mg/dL. LDL, the "bad" cholesterol, is ideally under 100 for most people, with LDL of 190 or more prompting treatment on its own. HDL, the "good" one, is considered low under 40 in men and under 50 in women, and triglycerides should be under 150. Decisions about statins rely on overall cardiovascular risk (age, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, family history, and LDL), not on a single number. Common statin side effects are muscle aches, and a clinician should be called for severe muscle pain or dark urine.

## Reading a complete blood count (CBC)
Source: Mayo Clinic Laboratories and standard hematology reference ranges
Keywords: cbc, complete blood count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, anemia, white blood cells, wbc, platelets, lab results, blood test

Typical adult ranges are approximately: hemoglobin 13.5 to 17.5 g/dL for men and 12.0 to 15.5 for women, white blood cells 4,500 to 11,000 per microliter, and platelets 150,000 to 450,000. Low hemoglobin means anemia, most commonly from iron deficiency, blood loss, or chronic disease, and its cause needs to be found. A high white count often reflects infection, inflammation, or stress, and a very low one can raise infection risk. A low platelet count raises bleeding risk and a very high one raises clotting risk. Ranges vary slightly between laboratories, and a result marked just outside the range is not necessarily a problem, so it should always be read by the ordering clinician in context.

## Reading a basic metabolic panel and kidney function
Source: National Kidney Foundation; standard clinical chemistry reference ranges
Keywords: metabolic panel, bmp, cmp, sodium, potassium, creatinine, bun, egfr, kidney, ckd, electrolytes, lab results

Typical adult values are sodium 135 to 145 mEq/L, potassium 3.5 to 5.0, creatinine roughly 0.7 to 1.3 mg/dL in men and 0.6 to 1.1 in women, and BUN 7 to 20. Potassium above about 5.5 is concerning and 6.0 or more can be dangerous for the heart, and very low sodium can cause confusion and seizures. The eGFR estimates kidney filtration: 90 or more is normal, 60 to 89 mildly reduced, and below 60 for three months or more is chronic kidney disease. Many medicines, including NSAIDs and metformin, need dose changes or avoidance when eGFR is low. A rise in creatinine over days can indicate acute kidney injury, often from dehydration or medications, and needs prompt evaluation.

## Thyroid tests (TSH)
Source: American Thyroid Association guidelines
Keywords: thyroid, tsh, hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, levothyroxine, synthroid, fatigue, weight, free t4

A typical TSH range is about 0.4 to 4.0 mIU/L. A high TSH means the thyroid is underactive (hypothyroidism), causing fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, and dry skin, and it is treated with daily levothyroxine. A low TSH suggests an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism), with weight loss, tremor, palpitations, heat intolerance, and anxiety. Levothyroxine should be taken on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, and separated by about 4 hours from calcium and iron supplements.

## Normal adult vital signs
Source: American Heart Association; standard clinical references
Keywords: vital signs, heart rate, pulse, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, spo2, temperature, normal, resting, oximeter

For adults at rest, a normal heart rate is 60 to 100 beats per minute, breathing is 12 to 20 breaths per minute, oral temperature averages around 98.6 F (37 C) with a normal span from roughly 97 to 99.5, and oxygen saturation is 95 to 100 percent. A reading under 90 percent oxygen is a reason to get urgent care, and 90 to 94 should be discussed with a clinician promptly, especially with symptoms. Fingertip oximeters can read inaccurately with cold hands, nail polish, or darker skin tones, so symptoms matter more than a single number.

## Heart attack warning signs, including in women
Source: American Heart Association; National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute
Keywords: heart attack, myocardial infarction, chest pain, women, jaw pain, nausea, shortness of breath, angina, cardiac, symptoms

Typical signs are pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain in the center of the chest lasting more than a few minutes or coming and going, pain spreading to the arm, shoulder, neck, jaw, or back, shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea, and lightheadedness. Women, older adults, and people with diabetes more often have less typical symptoms, such as unusual fatigue, nausea or vomiting, jaw or back pain, or shortness of breath without much chest pain. Anyone suspecting a heart attack should call 911 rather than drive, because paramedics can begin treatment on the way. Do not wait to see if it passes.

## Stroke warning signs: BE-FAST
Source: American Stroke Association; National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke
Keywords: stroke, tia, face drooping, arm weakness, speech, be fast, brain attack, mini stroke, time is brain

BE-FAST stands for Balance loss, Eyes (sudden vision changes), Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, and Time to call 911. Other signs are a sudden severe headache, confusion, or numbness on one side of the body. Clot-busting treatment works only within a limited window, usually within about 4.5 hours of when symptoms started, which is why knowing the last time the person was normal matters. A transient ischemic attack (TIA), or mini-stroke, whose symptoms fade within minutes or hours, is still an emergency because it predicts a coming stroke.

## CPR and AED basics for bystanders
Source: American Heart Association Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care
Keywords: cpr, cardiac arrest, aed, defibrillator, compressions, unresponsive, not breathing, resuscitation, hands-only

If someone collapses, is unresponsive, and isn't breathing normally, call 911 or have someone else call, and begin hands-only CPR: push hard and fast in the center of the chest, about 2 inches (5 cm) deep, at 100 to 120 compressions a minute, letting the chest fully rise between pushes, and don't stop until help takes over. Send someone for an AED if one is nearby, turn it on, and follow its voice instructions; it will only shock a rhythm that needs it, and it is safe for untrained users. Rescue breaths matter more for children and drowning victims, but doing compressions alone is much better than doing nothing.

## Choking: what to do
Source: American Red Cross; American Heart Association
Keywords: choking, heimlich, airway obstruction, abdominal thrusts, back blows, infant choking, food stuck

If an adult or child can't cough, speak, or breathe, call 911 and give five firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of the hand, followed by five abdominal thrusts, and alternate until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive, at which point lower them and begin CPR. If they can cough forcefully, encourage coughing rather than interfering. For infants under one year, give five back blows with the baby face down along your forearm, then five chest thrusts using two fingers; do not use abdominal thrusts on infants.

## Anaphylaxis and epinephrine
Source: American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology; Food Allergy Research and Education
Keywords: anaphylaxis, epinephrine, epipen, allergic reaction, food allergy, peanut, bee sting, hives, swelling, auto-injector

Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction, usually within minutes to an hour of a food, insect sting, or medication, with hives, swelling of the lips or throat, wheezing, trouble breathing, vomiting, dizziness, or fainting. Epinephrine is the first-line treatment and should be given immediately into the outer thigh: 0.3 mg for people over about 30 kg (66 lb), and 0.15 mg for 15 to 30 kg. Call 911 even if symptoms improve, because a second wave can occur hours later. Antihistamines such as Benadryl do not treat anaphylaxis and should not delay epinephrine.

## Burns: first aid and when to get care
Source: American Burn Association; American Academy of Dermatology
Keywords: burn, scald, first degree, second degree, third degree, blister, ice, butter, chemical burn, sunburn, hot water

Cool a thermal burn with cool, not ice-cold, running water for about 20 minutes as soon as possible, remove rings and tight clothing before swelling, and cover loosely with a clean non-stick dressing. Don't use ice, butter, oil, or toothpaste, and don't pop blisters. Seek urgent or emergency care for burns on the face, hands, feet, groin, or over a joint, burns larger than the person's palm, deep burns that look white, leathery, or charred, chemical or electrical burns, burns from inhaling smoke, and any significant burn in a young child or older adult. Chemical burns need flushing with large amounts of water for at least 20 minutes.

## Sprains, strains, and when to get an X-ray
Source: American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons; Ottawa Ankle Rules
Keywords: sprain, strain, ankle, rice, swelling, bruise, fracture, broken bone, x-ray, ottawa ankle rules, weight bearing

For a mild sprain or strain, protect the area, rest it briefly, apply ice for 15 to 20 minutes at a time, use compression, and elevate it, then restart gentle movement early because prolonged rest slows recovery. An X-ray is warranted if there is bone tenderness at the back edge or tip of the ankle bones, or the person cannot take four steps right after the injury and in the clinic. Other signs of a possible fracture include deformity, severe pain with any movement, numbness, a pale or cold limb, or a bone visible through the skin, which is an emergency.

## Head injury and concussion danger signs
Source: CDC HEADS UP program; American Academy of Neurology
Keywords: concussion, head injury, head trauma, headache, vomiting, loss of consciousness, drowsy, pupils, brain injury, football

After a bump or blow to the head, go to the emergency department or call 911 for loss of consciousness, a headache that worsens, repeated vomiting, seizure, one pupil larger than the other, weakness or numbness, slurred speech, increasing confusion or agitation, unusual drowsiness or inability to wake, or clear fluid from the nose or ears. Mild concussion symptoms include headache, dizziness, nausea, sensitivity to light, and trouble concentrating. Rest briefly, then return gradually to normal activity, and avoid any activity risking another head injury until cleared by a clinician.

## Dehydration, vomiting, and diarrhea
Source: CDC; American Academy of Pediatrics; World Health Organization oral rehydration guidance
Keywords: dehydration, diarrhea, vomiting, stomach flu, gastroenteritis, oral rehydration, food poisoning, electrolytes, thirsty

Most vomiting and diarrhea from a stomach virus or food poisoning passes in a few days. Sip fluids frequently in small amounts, and use an oral rehydration solution (such as Pedialyte) rather than plain water, juice, or sports drinks alone, especially for children and older adults. Seek care for blood in stool or vomit, a fever above 102 F, inability to keep fluids down for a day, signs of dehydration (dark urine, very little urine, dizziness, dry mouth, sunken eyes, no tears in a child), severe belly pain, diarrhea lasting more than 3 days, or recent antibiotics. Avoid anti-diarrheal drugs such as loperamide when there is fever or bloody stool.

## Urinary tract infection (UTI) and kidney infection
Source: Infectious Diseases Society of America; American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists
Keywords: uti, urinary tract infection, bladder infection, burning urination, dysuria, frequency, pyelonephritis, kidney infection, cystitis, antibiotic

A bladder infection typically causes burning with urination, urgency, frequency, and lower abdominal discomfort, and it usually needs a short course of antibiotics prescribed after a clinician evaluates it. Fever, chills, back or flank pain, nausea, or vomiting suggest a kidney infection that needs prompt care, as does any UTI symptoms in pregnancy, in men, in children, or in someone with diabetes or a catheter. Drinking fluids helps comfort, and cranberry products may modestly reduce recurrences but don't treat an active infection.

## Sore throat and strep throat
Source: IDSA Group A Streptococcal Pharyngitis Guideline; CDC
Keywords: sore throat, strep, strep throat, pharyngitis, tonsils, rapid strep test, antibiotics, fever, swollen glands, cough

Most sore throats are viral and improve within a week with fluids, rest, throat lozenges, and acetaminophen or ibuprofen. Strep is more likely with sudden sore throat and fever, swollen tender neck glands, white patches on the tonsils, and no cough, and it is diagnosed with a rapid test or throat culture, since treating it with antibiotics prevents complications such as rheumatic fever. Urgent care is needed for drooling, inability to swallow, trouble breathing, a muffled "hot potato" voice, or a stiff, swollen neck, which may signal an abscess or epiglottitis.

## Sepsis warning signs
Source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; Surviving Sepsis Campaign
Keywords: sepsis, infection, septic shock, confusion, fast heart rate, low blood pressure, clammy, mottled skin, emergency

Sepsis is the body's life-threatening response to infection. Warning signs are an infection plus any combination of fever or unusually low temperature with shivering, fast heart rate, fast breathing, confusion or unusual sleepiness, clammy or mottled skin, very little urine, and extreme pain or feeling "the sickest I've ever felt." It is an emergency: call 911 or go to the emergency department and say you are worried about sepsis, because early antibiotics and fluids greatly improve survival. Older adults, infants, people with diabetes, cancer, or weakened immune systems, and those with recent surgery are at higher risk.

## Heat exhaustion versus heat stroke
Source: CDC; National Weather Service; American College of Sports Medicine
Keywords: heat stroke, heat exhaustion, heat, dehydration, hot weather, desert, sweating, hyperthermia, car, summer

Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, cramps, and cool clammy skin; move to a cool place, drink fluids, loosen clothing, and cool the skin with water or wet cloths. Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency with a body temperature of 103 F or higher, confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizures, or hot, red skin that may be dry or damp: call 911 and cool the person aggressively with cold water, ice packs to the neck, armpits, and groin, and a fan while waiting. Never leave a child or pet in a parked car. Older adults, young children, and people who exercise or work outdoors are most at risk.

## Snake, scorpion, and spider bites
Source: Poison Control; Wilderness Medical Society; CDC
Keywords: snakebite, rattlesnake, scorpion, black widow, brown recluse, spider bite, venom, antivenom, desert, bite

For a suspected rattlesnake bite, call 911, move away from the snake, keep the person calm and the bitten limb still at about heart level, and remove rings and tight items before swelling. Do not cut the wound, suck out venom, apply ice or a tight tourniquet, or give alcohol, and don't try to catch the snake. Antivenom at a hospital is the effective treatment. For scorpion stings, clean the area and apply a cool compress, but call Poison Control or go to the emergency room for severe pain, numbness, trouble breathing or swallowing, or in a young child. Black widow bites can cause severe cramping and need medical evaluation.

## Hypothermia and frostbite
Source: CDC; Wilderness Medical Society
Keywords: hypothermia, cold, frostbite, shivering, exposure, warm up, freezing, winter

Hypothermia is a core temperature under 95 F (35 C), with shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, and confusion, and later shivering stops. Move the person indoors, remove wet clothes, wrap them in blankets, warm the chest and core first with warm (not hot) drinks if they are alert, and call 911 for confusion or drowsiness. Frostbitten skin turns pale, waxy, or numb; warm it gently in warm, not hot, water and don't rub it or use direct heat, and don't rewarm if it could refreeze.

## Severe headache and migraine red flags
Source: American Headache Society; the SNOOP red-flag mnemonic; American College of Emergency Physicians
Keywords: headache, migraine, thunderclap, worst headache, stiff neck, tension headache, red flags, aura, cluster

Most headaches are tension-type or migraine and are not dangerous. Migraines typically cause one-sided throbbing pain with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound, sometimes with a visual aura. Get emergency care for a sudden "thunderclap" headache that peaks within a minute, the worst headache of your life, headache with fever and stiff neck, after a head injury, with confusion, weakness, vision loss, or trouble speaking, a new headache after age 50, or one that worsens when lying down or with cough, or in someone who is pregnant, on blood thinners, or has cancer or a weakened immune system. Frequent use of pain relievers (more than about 2 to 3 days a week) can itself cause rebound headaches.

## Low back pain red flags
Source: American College of Physicians; North American Spine Society
Keywords: back pain, sciatica, low back pain, cauda equina, numbness, bladder, bowel, leg weakness, spine, slipped disc

Most low back pain improves within about 4 to 6 weeks with staying active, heat, gentle movement, and over-the-counter pain relievers, and bed rest tends to make it worse. Urgent evaluation is needed for new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the groin or saddle area, rapidly worsening leg weakness, fever, pain after significant trauma, pain with a history of cancer, or unrelenting night pain. Numbness, tingling, or pain that runs below the knee after a lifting injury suggests sciatica and should be examined if it persists or worsens.

## Asthma attacks and rescue inhalers
Source: Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA); National Asthma Education and Prevention Program
Keywords: asthma, wheezing, inhaler, albuterol, rescue inhaler, shortness of breath, asthma attack, peak flow, spacer

During an attack, sit upright and use the rescue inhaler (albuterol), 2 to 4 puffs with a spacer if available, and repeat every 20 minutes up to three times in the first hour. Call 911 if breathing is not improving after the first treatments, if speaking in full sentences is difficult, if lips or fingertips turn blue, or if the inhaler is needed more often than every 4 hours. Needing a rescue inhaler more than twice a week suggests asthma is not well controlled and the person should discuss a daily controller medicine with their doctor.

## Pregnancy: medicines to avoid and warning signs
Source: American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists; FDA; CDC
Keywords: pregnancy, pregnant, prenatal, folic acid, preeclampsia, nsaids in pregnancy, medications in pregnancy, bleeding, fetal movement, morning sickness

Acetaminophen is the usual first choice for pain or fever in pregnancy, while NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and naproxen should generally be avoided, especially from 20 weeks on, and ACE inhibitors, ARBs, warfarin, and isotretinoin are known to harm a pregnancy. A daily folic acid supplement of 400 micrograms starting before conception reduces neural tube defects. Alcohol has no known safe amount in pregnancy. Call the obstetrician or go to the emergency department right away for vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, a severe headache, vision changes, sudden swelling of the face or hands (possible preeclampsia), fluid leaking, fever, persistent vomiting, or a clear decrease in the baby's movements. Anyone who might be pregnant should check with a clinician before starting any new medicine.

## Antibiotics: what they treat and how to use them
Source: CDC Be Antibiotic Aware campaign; Infectious Diseases Society of America
Keywords: antibiotics, antibiotic resistance, cold, flu, virus, bacterial infection, amoxicillin, penicillin allergy, finish course, diarrhea

Antibiotics treat bacterial infections only and do nothing against viruses such as colds, flu, most sore throats, and most coughs and bronchitis. Using them when not needed causes side effects such as diarrhea, rashes, and Clostridioides difficile infection and fuels resistant bacteria. They should be taken exactly as prescribed, not shared or saved, and the person should tell the prescriber about any past reaction; many reported "penicillin allergies" are not true allergies and can be formally tested. Severe diarrhea during or weeks after antibiotics, or hives, swelling, or trouble breathing, needs urgent medical attention.

## Opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, and naloxone
Source: CDC opioid prescribing guideline; FDA boxed warning on opioids with benzodiazepines; SAMHSA
Keywords: opioid, oxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl, naloxone, narcan, benzodiazepine, xanax, overdose, alcohol, respiratory depression, withdrawal

Opioids slow and can stop breathing, particularly when combined with benzodiazepines (such as alprazolam or lorazepam), alcohol, or sleep medicines, so the FDA boxed warning cautions against combining them. Signs of an opioid overdose are unresponsiveness, slow or absent breathing, blue lips, and tiny pupils. Give naloxone (Narcan nasal spray), call 911, and give a second dose after 2 to 3 minutes if there is no response; naloxone is safe to give even if it turns out not to be an opioid overdose. Stopping benzodiazepines or alcohol suddenly after heavy regular use can cause seizures and delirium, so it should be done under medical supervision.

## Alcohol: low-risk limits and dangerous withdrawal
Source: NIAAA; CDC; American Society of Addiction Medicine
Keywords: alcohol, drinking, binge, withdrawal, delirium tremens, alcohol use disorder, hangover, liver, quitting, drinks per day

Low-risk drinking guidelines say no more than 2 drinks a day for men and 1 for women, and no more than 14 and 7 per week respectively, while binge drinking means about 4 or 5 drinks within two hours. People who drink heavily every day should not stop abruptly on their own: withdrawal can cause tremor, sweating, hallucinations, seizures, and delirium tremens, which can be fatal, and requires medical supervision. Treatment is effective, and a clinician can discuss medications and counseling; SAMHSA's helpline at 1-800-662-4357 is free and open at all hours.

## Depression, anxiety, and when to get help
Source: American Psychiatric Association; National Institute of Mental Health; 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline
Keywords: depression, anxiety, panic, mental health, sad, hopeless, therapy, ssri, stress, 988, suicide, panic attack

Depression is more than sadness: it is two weeks or more of low mood or loss of interest plus changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, or feelings of worthlessness, and it is highly treatable with therapy, medication, or both. A panic attack causes sudden intense fear with a racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and dizziness, usually peaking within about 10 minutes, though a first episode with chest pain should be medically checked. Slow breathing (in for four seconds, out for six) and naming things you can see and hear can help ease it. Anyone thinking of suicide or self-harm should call or text 988 at any hour, or call 911 if in immediate danger.

## Nosebleeds, cuts, and wound care
Source: American Academy of Otolaryngology; American College of Emergency Physicians; CDC tetanus guidance
Keywords: nosebleed, epistaxis, cut, wound, laceration, stitches, tetanus, bleeding, wash, infection, scrape

For a nosebleed, sit up and lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for 10 to 15 minutes without checking, and don't tip the head back; seek care if it lasts more than 20 minutes or follows a head injury. For a cut, apply direct pressure, then rinse with clean running water and cover it. Stitches are usually needed for gaping wounds, deep cuts, or those over a joint or on the face, ideally within about 12 hours. A tetanus booster is advised if the last one was over 10 years ago, or over 5 years ago for a dirty wound. Spreading redness, warmth, pus, red streaks, or fever suggest infection.

## Eye injuries and chemical splashes
Source: American Academy of Ophthalmology
Keywords: eye, chemical in eye, eye injury, red eye, vision loss, pink eye, foreign object, flashes, floaters, eye pain

For a chemical splash in the eye, immediately flush with clean water for at least 15 to 20 minutes, holding the eyelid open, then seek emergency care. Sudden vision loss, a curtain or shadow over the vision, new flashes of light with a shower of floaters, severe eye pain, a penetrating injury, or light sensitivity with a red painful eye need same-day emergency evaluation. Don't rub the eye or try to remove an embedded object. Most pink eye is viral or allergic and settles in a week or so, but thick discharge with pain or vision change should be examined.

## Cancer screening basics
Source: US Preventive Services Task Force; American Cancer Society
Keywords: screening, colonoscopy, mammogram, pap smear, cervical cancer, lung cancer screening, prostate, psa, colorectal, breast cancer, preventive care

Current USPSTF recommendations are: colorectal cancer screening for ages 45 to 75, mammograms every two years for women ages 40 to 74, cervical screening for ages 21 to 65, and annual low-dose CT lung screening for adults 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year smoking history who smoke now or quit within 15 years. Prostate PSA testing from 55 to 69 is an individual decision made with a clinician after discussing benefits and harms. Guidance changes over time and starts earlier with a strong family history or genetic risk, so each person should confirm their own schedule with their clinician.

## Adult vaccines worth knowing
Source: CDC Adult Immunization Schedule
Keywords: vaccine, flu shot, tdap, shingles, shingrix, pneumonia, pneumococcal, covid, hepatitis b, immunization, booster

The CDC adult schedule generally includes a yearly influenza vaccine, a Tdap once and then a tetanus-diphtheria booster every 10 years, the two-dose recombinant shingles vaccine (Shingrix) from age 50, a pneumococcal vaccine from age 50 (or earlier with certain conditions), updated COVID-19 vaccination as recommended each season, and hepatitis B for most adults through 59. Recommendations are updated regularly, and pregnancy, immune conditions, and travel can change the list, so the current schedule should be confirmed with a clinician or pharmacist.

## Sleep and insomnia
Source: American Academy of Sleep Medicine; American College of Physicians
Keywords: sleep, insomnia, sleeping pills, melatonin, cbt-i, sleep hygiene, tired, fatigue, sleep apnea, snoring

Adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night. The most effective long-term treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which beats sleeping pills in the long run. Helpful habits include a regular wake time, limiting caffeine after midday, keeping the bedroom cool and dark, getting out of bed if unable to sleep after about 20 minutes, and avoiding alcohol close to bedtime, which fragments sleep. Loud snoring with gasping, witnessed pauses in breathing, and heavy daytime sleepiness suggest sleep apnea, which should be evaluated.

## Tick bites and Lyme disease
Source: CDC Lyme disease guidance; IDSA
Keywords: tick, tick bite, lyme disease, bullseye rash, erythema migrans, doxycycline, rocky mountain spotted fever, outdoors

Remove an attached tick as soon as possible by grasping it close to the skin with fine-tipped tweezers and pulling straight up with steady pressure, then clean the area. Lyme disease is more likely when the tick has been attached for 36 hours or more. An expanding red rash, sometimes with a clear center, appears in many cases within 3 to 30 days, along with fever, fatigue, and joint aches, and it is treated with antibiotics such as doxycycline. See a clinician for any rash, fever, or flu-like illness after a tick bite, as other tick-borne diseases, such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever, also need prompt treatment.
