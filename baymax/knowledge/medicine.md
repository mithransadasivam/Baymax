## CAR T-cell therapy produces durable remissions in aggressive lymphoma
Source: Locke et al., 2019 (ZUMA-1 trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1804980; supported by Schuster et al. (JULIET trial), NEJM 2019
Keywords: cancer, oncology, car t-cell, chimeric antigen receptor, lymphoma, immunotherapy, leukemia, cell therapy, cytokine release syndrome

Engineering a patient's own T-cells to recognize the CD19 protein on cancerous B-cells produced long-lasting remissions in patients with aggressive large B-cell lymphoma who had already failed multiple prior treatments, with roughly a third to half of patients still in remission years later in follow-up studies. This was one of the first proofs that genetically engineered "living" cell therapies could work against cancer, not just theoretical models. The treatment carries serious risks including cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity, requires specialized centers to administer, and works far better in blood cancers than it has so far in solid tumors like breast or lung cancer.

## Pembrolizumab outperforms older immunotherapy in advanced melanoma
Source: Robert et al., 2015 (KEYNOTE-006 trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1503093
Keywords: cancer, melanoma, immunotherapy, checkpoint inhibitor, pd-1, pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, immune system

In a large randomized trial, the PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab significantly extended both progression-free and overall survival in advanced melanoma compared with ipilimumab, the prior standard immunotherapy, while causing fewer severe side effects. This helped establish PD-1 blockade as a first-line option for metastatic melanoma and paved the way for checkpoint inhibitors across many other cancer types. Response is not universal — a substantial fraction of patients do not respond or eventually progress, and predicting who will benefit remains an active area of research.

## Adjuvant pembrolizumab reduces melanoma recurrence after surgery
Source: Eggermont et al., 2018 -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1802357
Keywords: cancer, melanoma, immunotherapy, checkpoint inhibitor, pembrolizumab, adjuvant therapy, recurrence, surgery

Giving pembrolizumab for about a year after surgical removal of high-risk stage III melanoma significantly lowered the rate of cancer recurrence compared with placebo, with the benefit persisting in five-year follow-up data. This shifted practice toward treating patients earlier, before metastatic spread is detectable, rather than waiting for relapse. The tradeoff is that many patients who would never have relapsed anyway are exposed to the drug's autoimmune-type side effects, and it's not yet fully clear which patients most need this extra treatment.

## Semaglutide lowers cardiovascular risk in people with obesity, independent of diabetes
Source: Lincoff et al., 2023 (SELECT trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2307563
Keywords: obesity, weight loss, glp-1, semaglutide, ozempic, wegovy, cardiovascular disease, heart attack, stroke, metabolic disease

In over 17,000 adults with obesity or overweight and existing cardiovascular disease but without diabetes, weekly semaglutide injections reduced the combined risk of cardiovascular death, heart attack, or stroke by 20% compared with placebo, alongside about a 9% average body weight loss. This was the first strong evidence that a GLP-1 drug's heart benefits aren't just a side effect of treating diabetes. Gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, diarrhea) are common, long-term safety beyond a few years is still being tracked, and the trial doesn't address what happens to weight or risk after people stop the medication (weight regain is well documented in shorter studies).

## Lecanemab modestly slows cognitive decline in early Alzheimer's disease
Source: van Dyck et al., 2023 (CLARITY AD trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2212948
Keywords: alzheimer's disease, dementia, amyloid, lecanemab, leqembi, monoclonal antibody, cognitive decline, neurodegeneration

Lecanemab, an antibody that clears amyloid-beta plaques from the brain, slowed the rate of cognitive and functional decline by about 27% relative to placebo over 18 months in people with early, mild Alzheimer's disease. This was the first phase 3 trial to convincingly show that removing amyloid translates into a clinically measurable (if modest) benefit, ending decades of uncertainty about the "amyloid hypothesis." The effect size is small in absolute terms and may not be noticeable to patients or families day-to-day, and the drug carries a real risk of brain swelling or bleeding (ARIA), requiring MRI monitoring.

## Donanemab shows similar amyloid-clearing benefit to lecanemab in Alzheimer's
Source: Sims et al., 2023 (TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial) -- JAMA, DOI:10.1001/jama.2023.13239
Keywords: alzheimer's disease, dementia, amyloid, donanemab, kisunla, monoclonal antibody, cognitive decline

Donanemab slowed clinical decline in early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease by roughly 35% compared with placebo over 18 months, with the strongest effect in patients who had lower levels of a co-occurring brain protein called tau. Combined with lecanemab's results, this reinforced that anti-amyloid antibodies produce a real, if incremental, disease-modifying effect rather than just clearing plaques with no clinical consequence. Like lecanemab, it carries meaningful risk of brain swelling/bleeding, and neither drug reverses damage that has already occurred — they slow future decline, they don't restore lost function.

## The first mRNA COVID-19 vaccine showed 95% efficacy in its pivotal trial
Source: Polack et al., 2020 (Pfizer-BioNTech BNT162b2) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2034577
Keywords: covid-19, vaccine, mrna, pfizer, biontech, coronavirus, infectious disease, immunization

In a trial of about 44,000 participants, the Pfizer-BioNTech mRNA vaccine reduced symptomatic COVID-19 by 95% relative to placebo, with only mild to moderate short-term side effects (fatigue, injection-site pain). This was the first large-scale proof that mRNA vaccine technology — until then unproven in humans at scale — could work quickly and effectively against a novel pathogen, reshaping vaccine development for future outbreaks. Efficacy against symptomatic infection waned over months and against newer variants, which is why later boosters were needed; the original trial did not and could not measure very long-term protection or rare long-term side effects.

## CRISPR gene editing produces a functional cure for severe sickle cell disease
Source: Frangoul et al., 2024 (exa-cel / Casgevy) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2309676
Keywords: sickle cell disease, crispr, gene editing, exa-cel, casgevy, gene therapy, hemoglobin, blood disorder

Exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel) uses CRISPR-Cas9 to edit a patient's own blood stem cells so they resume producing fetal hemoglobin, which compensates for the defective adult hemoglobin that causes sickle cell disease. In the pivotal trial, 29 of 30 evaluable patients were free of severe pain crises for at least a year after treatment, and it became the first CRISPR-based therapy approved for human use. The process requires chemotherapy-like conditioning to make room in the bone marrow, which carries real risks including infertility, and the treatment is enormously expensive and currently available only at specialized centers — access, not efficacy, is now the main open problem.

## SGLT2 inhibitors reduce heart failure hospitalization and death, even without diabetes
Source: McMurray et al., 2019 (DAPA-HF) and Packer et al., 2020 (EMPEROR-Reduced) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1911303 and DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2022190
Keywords: heart failure, sglt2 inhibitor, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, cardiology, diabetes, hospitalization

Drugs originally developed to lower blood sugar by causing the kidneys to excrete glucose (SGLT2 inhibitors) were found to meaningfully reduce hospitalizations for heart failure and cardiovascular death in patients with reduced ejection fraction, regardless of whether they had diabetes. Across the two pivotal trials, the combined risk of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization dropped by around a quarter. This repurposing is now one of the pillars of modern heart failure treatment; the exact mechanism of cardiac benefit (distinct from the blood-sugar effect) is still not completely settled.

## RSV vaccines cut severe respiratory illness in older adults by over 80%
Source: Papi et al., 2023 (GSK RSV vaccine) and Walsh et al., 2023 (Pfizer RSVpreF) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2213836
Keywords: rsv, respiratory syncytial virus, vaccine, older adults, elderly, pneumonia, respiratory infection

Two large placebo-controlled trials found that new prefusion-F protein RSV vaccines reduced RSV-associated lower respiratory tract disease in adults 60 and older by roughly 80-83%, leading to their approval — the first vaccines ever available against a virus that had circulated, unaddressed, for decades. RSV causes tens of thousands of hospitalizations in older US adults each year, comparable to flu in severity. Efficacy in the second season after vaccination is lower than the first, so how often boosters will be needed is still being worked out.

## A new low-dose malaria vaccine shows strong efficacy in African children
Source: Datoo et al., 2024 (R21/Matrix-M phase 3) -- The Lancet, DOI:10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02511-4
Keywords: malaria, vaccine, r21, matrix-m, rts,s, africa, children, infectious disease, parasite

The R21/Matrix-M vaccine, given alongside seasonal antimalarial drugs or on its own, showed strong protection against clinical malaria in young children in multi-country African trials, and unlike the earlier RTS,S vaccine it can be manufactured cheaply at large scale. Both R21 and RTS,S are now WHO-prequalified and being rolled out, marking the first real vaccine tools against a disease that kills several hundred thousand children a year. Protection wanes over time and is well below 100%, so the vaccines are additive to bed nets and antimalarial drugs, not a replacement for them.

## Thrombectomy helps stroke patients even with large, already-established brain injury
Source: Costalat et al., 2024 (LASTE trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2314063
Keywords: stroke, thrombectomy, large vessel occlusion, neurology, brain infarct, emergency medicine

Mechanically removing a clot from a blocked brain artery (endovascular thrombectomy) improved functional outcomes and reduced mortality even in stroke patients with large amounts of already-infarcted brain tissue on imaging — a group previously thought to be past the point where the procedure would help. This significantly expanded the pool of stroke patients considered eligible for the procedure. The benefit is still a matter of degree, not a cure — many patients in this large-infarct group remain significantly disabled even after successful thrombectomy, and there's an increased risk of bleeding into the damaged tissue.

## An AI system can autonomously diagnose diabetic eye disease without a specialist
Source: Abramoff et al., 2018 (IDx-DR pivotal trial) -- npj Digital Medicine, DOI:10.1038/s41746-018-0040-6
Keywords: artificial intelligence, machine learning, diabetic retinopathy, diagnosis, screening, ophthalmology, deep learning, fda

In a primary-care trial, an AI system analyzing retinal photographs detected diabetic retinopathy with 87% sensitivity and 91% specificity, leading the FDA to authorize it in 2018 as the first-ever autonomous AI diagnostic device in any field of medicine — meaning it can render a diagnosis without a doctor reviewing the image. This opened the door to bringing specialist-level screening to clinics that have no eye specialist on site. The system is narrow: it only screens for one disease from one type of image, and it still refers uncertain or lower-quality images to a human specialist rather than clearing them itself.

## A single dose of psilocybin eased treatment-resistant depression for weeks
Source: Goodwin et al., 2022 -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2206443
Keywords: psilocybin, depression, mental health, psychedelic, treatment-resistant depression, psychiatry

In a phase 2 trial, a single 25mg dose of synthetic psilocybin (a psychoactive compound from "magic mushrooms"), given together with psychological support, produced a rapid and statistically significant reduction in depressive symptoms compared with a near-zero control dose, with some benefit persisting up to 12 weeks in some patients. This added to growing evidence that psychedelic-assisted therapy can act faster than conventional antidepressants for some patients. The trial was relatively small and short, psilocybin is still a controlled substance in most places outside clinical trials, and larger phase 3 trials are still needed to confirm durability and identify who responds best.

## Tirzepatide produced the largest average weight loss yet seen in an obesity drug trial
Source: Jastreboff et al., 2022 (SURMOUNT-1 trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2206038
Keywords: obesity, weight loss, tirzepatide, mounjaro, zepbound, glp-1, gip, metabolic disease

Tirzepatide, a drug that activates both the GLP-1 and GIP hormone receptors, produced average weight losses of 16-22.5% of body weight over 72 weeks depending on dose, substantially more than semaglutide alone had shown, and a head-to-head trial later confirmed tirzepatide outperforms semaglutide for weight loss. This class of drugs has changed obesity from a condition with few effective medical options into one with multiple strong ones. Nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting are common (though usually mild-to-moderate), long-term (beyond a few years) safety data are still accumulating, and weight tends to return after stopping the drug.

## A JAK inhibitor for rheumatoid arthritis carries higher cardiovascular and cancer risk than older biologics
Source: Ytterberg et al., 2022 (ORAL Surveillance trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2109927
Keywords: rheumatoid arthritis, jak inhibitor, tofacitinib, autoimmune disease, cardiovascular risk, cancer risk, biologics

In a large safety trial required by regulators, tofacitinib (a JAK inhibitor used for rheumatoid arthritis) was compared against older TNF-inhibitor biologics in patients over 50 with at least one cardiovascular risk factor, and it failed to meet the bar of "non-inferiority" — meaning it caused significantly more major cardiovascular events and cancers than the comparison drug. This led global regulators to add boxed warnings and restrict use of the entire JAK inhibitor class to patients who've failed other options first. It's an important reminder that a drug working well for its primary purpose doesn't guarantee it's equally safe compared to older alternatives — the finding is specific to this higher-risk population and dose, and the field is still working out which patients face the least added risk.

## A CRISPR "base editing" therapy showed early success at permanently lowering cholesterol
Source: Verve Therapeutics (VERVE-101), 2026 phase 1 trial -- Nature Medicine, DOI:10.1038/s41591-026-04254-4
Keywords: crispr, base editing, cholesterol, ldl, pcsk9, familial hypercholesterolemia, gene therapy, heart disease

A one-time infusion designed to make a single, permanent DNA edit that inactivates the PCSK9 gene in liver cells lowered LDL ("bad") cholesterol in the first six patients treated for a genetic high-cholesterol condition, without serious safety problems detected so far. This is one of the earliest human demonstrations of "base editing" — a more surgical form of CRISPR that changes one DNA letter rather than cutting DNA strands — working as a potential one-and-done treatment instead of a lifelong daily pill. This is a very small, early-stage (phase 1) trial with no control group and no long-term follow-up yet; permanence is also the flip side of the appeal, since an editing mistake or later-discovered problem cannot be undone.

## Evolocumab, a PCSK9-inhibiting antibody, further reduces heart attacks on top of statins
Source: Sabatine et al., 2017 (FOURIER trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1615664
Keywords: cholesterol, pcsk9 inhibitor, evolocumab, statin, cardiovascular disease, heart attack, ldl

Adding evolocumab, an injectable antibody that blocks the PCSK9 protein, to statin therapy in over 27,000 patients with existing cardiovascular disease lowered LDL cholesterol dramatically and reduced the combined risk of cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke, or related events by 15% over about 2.2 years. It confirmed that pushing LDL cholesterol far below traditional targets keeps producing benefit, with no clear floor. The absolute risk reduction (1.5 percentage points) is more modest than the relative one suggests, the drugs are expensive injectables rather than pills, and the trial was too short to fully assess very long-term safety of extremely low LDL levels.

## "Artificial pancreas" closed-loop systems improve blood sugar control in type 1 diabetes
Source: Brown et al., 2019 -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1907863; extended to young children by Ware et al. (PEDAP trial), NEJM 2022
Keywords: type 1 diabetes, artificial pancreas, closed-loop insulin, continuous glucose monitor, insulin pump, glycemic control

Automated "closed-loop" systems that use a continuous glucose sensor to algorithmically adjust an insulin pump in real time kept patients' blood sugar within the healthy target range significantly more of the time than standard sensor-augmented pumps, across trials spanning young children through adults. This meaningfully reduces the daily burden of manual diabetes management and lowers risk of dangerous high and low blood sugar swings. The systems still require carbohydrate counting and manual mealtime boluses in most current models (they are not fully autonomous), and cost/insurance access remains a barrier for many patients.

## Transcatheter aortic valve replacement matches surgery even in low-risk patients
Source: Mack et al., 2019 (PARTNER 3) and Popma et al., 2019 (Evolut Low Risk) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1814052 and DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1816885
Keywords: tavr, aortic stenosis, heart valve, cardiology, transcatheter, surgery, cardiac device

Two large randomized trials found that replacing a diseased aortic valve through a catheter (TAVR) rather than open-heart surgery produced equal or better one-year outcomes — including lower rates of death, stroke, and rehospitalization — even in patients who were healthy enough to safely undergo traditional surgery. This dramatically expanded TAVR's use beyond its original frail, high-risk population to become a mainstream option. Longer-term durability of the transcatheter valves (10+ years) in younger, low-risk patients who will live with the valve for decades is still being studied, since surgical valves have a longer track record.

## A personalized mRNA cancer vaccine plus immunotherapy reduced melanoma recurrence
Source: Merck/Moderna, 2026 (INTerpath-001 / KEYNOTE-942 phase 3, preliminary results) -- reported via company/press disclosures pending full peer-reviewed publication
Keywords: mrna vaccine, cancer vaccine, melanoma, neoantigen, pembrolizumab, personalized medicine, immunotherapy

A vaccine custom-built for each patient — encoding up to 34 mutated proteins unique to their own tumor — combined with the checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab, met its primary goal of improving recurrence-free survival in resected stage IIB-IV melanoma in a large phase 3 trial, the first positive late-stage result for both an mRNA cancer therapy and an individualized "neoantigen" vaccine. This validates a personalized-medicine approach that could in principle extend to other cancer types. As of these results, full peer-reviewed data and long-term follow-up were not yet published, so this should be treated as a promising but not fully mature result pending independent review.

## Epstein-Barr virus infection appears to be a necessary trigger for multiple sclerosis
Source: Bjornevik et al., 2022 -- Science, DOI:10.1126/science.abj8222
Keywords: multiple sclerosis, epstein-barr virus, ebv, autoimmune disease, neurology, virus, causation

Tracking blood samples from over 10 million US military members over 20 years, researchers found that the risk of developing multiple sclerosis rose more than 30-fold after infection with Epstein-Barr virus, while showing no comparable rise after other common viral infections — strong evidence that EBV is a necessary (though not sufficient) cause of MS, one of the largest causal findings in autoimmune disease research to date. This raises the possibility that an EBV vaccine or antiviral could eventually prevent or treat MS. It remains unclear why only a small fraction of the huge number of people infected with EBV (nearly everyone, eventually) go on to develop MS, so other genetic or environmental factors clearly matter too.

## Fecal microbiota transplant cures recurrent C. difficile infection far better than antibiotics
Source: van Nood et al., 2013 -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1205037
Keywords: fecal microbiota transplant, fmt, c. difficile, gut microbiome, antibiotic resistance, infectious disease

In a randomized trial stopped early because the effect was so clear, infusing stool from a healthy donor into the intestines of patients with recurrent, antibiotic-resistant C. difficile infection cured about 81% of patients after one infusion, compared with roughly 31% cured by the antibiotic vancomycin alone. This was the trial that turned fecal transplant from a fringe idea into a recognized, guideline-supported treatment, and it helped launch broader interest in the gut microbiome's role in disease. FMT for C. diff is now well-established, but its use for other conditions (IBD, obesity, autism, etc.) remains experimental, and screening donor stool for transmissible pathogens is an ongoing safety concern — a rare but serious transmission of drug-resistant bacteria via FMT has been documented.

## Lab-grown dopamine neurons show early safety and possible benefit in Parkinson's disease
Source: Takahashi et al., 2025 (Kyoto University phase I/II trial) -- Nature, DOI:10.1038/s41586-025-08700-0
Keywords: parkinson's disease, stem cells, ipsc, induced pluripotent stem cells, dopamine, regenerative medicine, cell transplant

Researchers transplanted dopamine-producing neurons, grown from reprogrammed stem cells (iPSCs), directly into the brains of Parkinson's patients in an early-phase trial; the cells survived, produced dopamine, and did not form tumors, though the trial mainly measured safety rather than definitive symptom improvement. This is one of the first real-world tests of using "made-to-order" replacement brain cells for a neurodegenerative disease rather than a drug. The trial was small (about 7 patients) with only mild-to-moderate side effects reported; whether this approach meaningfully improves motor symptoms or slows disease progression needs confirmation in larger, controlled trials.

## Long COVID affects a meaningful minority of infections and involves multiple biological mechanisms
Source: Sudre et al., 2021 -- Nature Medicine, DOI:10.1038/s41591-021-01292-y; mechanisms reviewed in Nature Medicine 2024, DOI:10.1038/s41591-024-03173-6
Keywords: long covid, post-viral syndrome, fatigue, chronic illness, immune dysregulation, covid-19

Population studies found that around 13% of people with COVID-19 report symptoms lasting a month or more, with a smaller fraction (roughly 2-4%) having symptoms persisting three months or longer, and risk is higher with older age, higher body weight, female sex, and having more symptoms during the initial infection. Proposed mechanisms include lingering viral fragments, immune system dysregulation, small blood vessel inflammation, and disrupted gut microbiome balance, but no single mechanism explains all cases. This remains a genuinely unsettled area: there is no universally agreed case definition, no validated diagnostic test, and no proven cure — current management is symptom-focused rather than curative.

## GPT-4 matches or exceeds average physician accuracy on difficult diagnostic cases, but not reliably
Source: Kanjee, Crowe, and Rodman, 2023 -- JAMA, DOI:10.1001/jama.2023.8288
Keywords: artificial intelligence, large language model, gpt-4, chatgpt, medical diagnosis, clinical decision support

When tested against 70 challenging diagnostic cases published in NEJM's case-record series, GPT-4 included the correct diagnosis somewhere in its top suggestions about 64% of the time and ranked it first about 39% of the time — comparable to, and in some benchmarks better than, average physician performance on the same notoriously hard cases. This sparked serious interest in AI as a diagnostic aid for rare or complex presentations. These are curated "zebra" cases meant to be hard and interesting, not representative of everyday clinical practice, the model can also confidently state wrong answers, and no regulatory body has approved an LLM as an autonomous diagnostician — current consensus treats it as a second opinion tool, not a replacement for a physician.

## A long-acting injectable HIV prevention drug outperforms daily pills
Source: Landovitz et al., 2021 (HPTN 083 trial) -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2101016
Keywords: hiv, prep, pre-exposure prophylaxis, cabotegravir, infectious disease, prevention

An injection of long-acting cabotegravir given every two months reduced new HIV infections by about 66% more than daily oral PrEP pills (tenofovir/emtricitabine) among men who have sex with men and transgender women, mainly because it solves the adherence problem of remembering a daily pill. This was a major advance in HIV prevention, especially for people who struggle with daily medication routines. A downside is that if someone becomes infected despite the injections (a rare but real event), the slowly-clearing drug can promote resistance to an entire class of HIV medications, complicating later treatment.

## Gene therapy restores hearing in children born with a specific genetic deafness
Source: Lv et al., 2024 and follow-up study, Nature 2026 -- NEJM, DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa2400521
Keywords: gene therapy, hearing loss, deafness, otof gene, congenital deafness, cochlea

A single injection into the inner ear, delivering a working copy of the OTOF gene, restored substantial hearing and speech perception in children born deaf due to OTOF mutations — a rare but well-understood genetic cause responsible for roughly 2-8% of congenital deafness cases. Extended follow-up out to at least 2.5 years in a larger cohort showed the improvements were sustained, not just a short-term effect. This gene therapy only works for this one specific genetic cause of deafness, not deafness broadly (which has many different causes), and long-term (decade-plus) durability and safety are still being followed.

## Metformin's ability to slow human aging is a live hypothesis, not yet a proven fact
Source: Barzilai et al., 2016 (TAME trial concept) -- Cell Metabolism, DOI:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.05.011; trial ongoing as of 2025-2026
Keywords: metformin, aging, longevity, tame trial, healthspan, geroscience, biological age

The TAME (Targeting Aging with Metformin) trial is a large, NIH-supported study designed to test whether the cheap, decades-old diabetes drug metformin can delay the onset of aging-related diseases (heart disease, cancer, dementia) in non-diabetic older adults; it is still enrolling/ongoing and has not yet reported final results. Smaller studies suggest metformin may favorably shift biological-age markers and gut microbiome composition, activating cellular pathways linked to longevity in animal models. It is important to be clear that no large human trial has yet proven metformin extends lifespan or healthspan in people without diabetes — much of the public enthusiasm for metformin as an anti-aging drug outpaces the current evidence, which remains preliminary and observational.

## Gut bacteria composition predicts and may causally affect response to cancer immunotherapy
Source: Gopalakrishnan et al., 2018 -- Science, DOI:10.1126/science.aan4236
Keywords: gut microbiome, immunotherapy, checkpoint inhibitor, melanoma, cancer, fecal transplant, ruminococcaceae

Melanoma patients who responded well to PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy had a more diverse gut microbiome and higher levels of specific bacteria (notably Ruminococcaceae) than non-responders, and transplanting stool from responding patients into germ-free mice improved the mice's anti-tumor immune response — suggesting the gut microbiome doesn't just correlate with, but may actively shape, immunotherapy success. This opened a new therapeutic angle: modifying the microbiome (via diet, probiotics, or fecal transplant) to improve cancer treatment response, and early human trials have shown fecal transplants can re-sensitize some non-responding patients. The exact bacterial species or mechanisms responsible aren't fully pinned down yet, and results vary somewhat between different research cohorts, so a standardized microbiome-based intervention isn't yet ready for routine clinical use.
